# Sea Urchin Name History — From Greek Hedgehog to English Urchin

## Summary

"Sea urchin" means **"hedgehog of the sea."** Both the English common name and the
scientific name say that independently, through two different language families. The
animal is named for its spines, which reminded Europeans of a hedgehog — and the word
for hedgehog is also, in English, the word for a ragged child.

The chain in one line:

```
PIE *h₁eǵʰis "hedgehog"
  ├── Greek ἐχῖνος ekhînos "hedgehog, sea urchin" → Latin echīnus → Echinoidea (class)
  └── Latin ērīcius "hedgehog" → Vulgar Latin *ērīciōnem → Old North French (h)erichon
        └── Middle English urchoun / yrchoun
              ├── "hedgehog" → sea hedgehog → sea urchin (1590s)
              └── "hedgehog-like creature" → mischievous elf → street child (16c.)
```

## Details

### 1. The Greek root: ἐχῖνος, the word that means both animals

Ancient Greek ἐχῖνος (*ekhînos*, masculine, second declension) carries a cluster of
senses, in LSJ's order: **hedgehog; sea urchin; the shell of a sea urchin; jug, cup,
vase; nut shell (e.g. chestnut); the omasum (third stomach of ruminants); the moulding
atop a Doric capital**
([Wiktionary: ἐχῖνος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BC%90%CF%87%E1%BF%96%CE%BD%CE%BF%CF%82)).

The extension is morphological: the classical world named anything spiky, hollow, or
dome-with-teeth after the hedgehog, a pot and an architectural moulding included. That
is why *echinus* in English architectural vocabulary still names an ovolo moulding,
unrelated to the animal.

Greek never needed a "sea hedgehog" coinage, because *ekhînos* already covered both
animals. English did need one, and built it.

Etymology: Proto-Hellenic \**ekʰinos*, from PIE \**h₁eǵʰis* "hedgehog, hedgehog-like
animals", with cognates in Mycenaean Greek 𐀁𐀑𐀜 *e-ki-no*, Old Armenian *ozni*,
Proto-Germanic \**igilaz*, and Albanian *esh* (Beekes, *Etymological Dictionary of
Greek*, 2010, p. 489, as cited by
[Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BC%90%CF%87%E1%BF%96%CE%BD%CE%BF%CF%82)).
Latin borrowed it as *echīnus*, giving *echino-*, the class **Echinoidea** (Leske,
1778), the genus *Echinus*, and *echinoderm* — literally "spiny-skinned"
([Wikipedia: Sea urchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_urchin)).

### 2. The Latin branch: ērīcius and the French -on suffix

A second, independent hedgehog word ran in parallel:

- Latin **ērīcius** "hedgehog", also "a kind of military obstacle" — the spiked barrier
  sense, another instance of hedgehog-as-spiky-thing
  ([Merriam-Webster: urchin](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/urchin)).
- A derivative with the Latin personal suffix *-ōn-*: Vulgar Latin \**ērīciōn-*.
- Old North French **(h)erichon**, Picard *irchon* / *iršõ*, Walloon *urechon*.
- Middle English **yrchoun / urcheoun / hirichoun / urchoun**, glossed "hedgehog, sea
  urchin", first recorded c. 1300–1350
  ([Wiktionary: urchin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/urchin);
  [Dictionary.com: urchin](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/urchin);
  [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/urchin)).

English took the **northern** French form, not the Parisian one; Merriam-Webster notes
the Anglo-French borrowing "clearly reflects a northern French form with a hushing
consonant", comparing Walloon *urechon, irchon* and Picard *iršõ*
([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/urchin)).

Modern French split the word. Standard French *hérisson* is the hedgehog, from *ericius*
with a late expressive initial *h*. The sea urchin in French is **oursin**, which the
*Trésor de la langue française* derives from *ours* "bear" — "bear fur", then "bear-fur
hat", then the animal. A different metaphor entirely, and not a relative of *hérisson*
despite the resemblance ([Language Hat: Urchin](https://languagehat.com/urchin/)).

English later imported the Parisian form too, as the rare doublet **herisson**
([Wiktionary: urchin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/urchin)).

### 3. The word meant hedgehog first — and still does, regionally

Merriam-Webster marks the hedgehog sense *archaic* and notes that *hedgehog* has
"largely replaced" it in standard British and North American English. The *Survey of
English Dialects* still recorded *urchin*, including variants such as *prickly-urchin*,
in the west Midlands and north of England in the 1950s
([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/urchin)). Collins lists it
as "an archaic or dialect name for a hedgehog"
([Collins](https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/urchin)). Ulster-Scots
preserves *hurchin* as a hedgehog word, a Norman loan surviving at the periphery
([Language Hat](https://languagehat.com/urchin/)).

Shakespeare uses *urchin* for hedgehog in *The Tempest* and *Titus Andronicus*, and once,
in *Merry Wives of Windsor* IV.4, for a goblin or fairy
([Language Hat](https://languagehat.com/urchin/)).

### 4. "Sea urchin" is the late arrival, 1590s

The compound **sea urchin** is recorded from the 1590s: Merriam-Webster gives the first
known use as **1591**, Dictionary.com dates it **1585–1595**
([Merriam-Webster: sea urchin](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/sea%20urchin);
[Dictionary.com: sea urchin](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/sea-urchin)).

Before that the transparent English phrasing was **sea hedgehog**, now archaic and listed
as such, alongside the outdated synonyms *sea egg*, *sea chestnut* (French
*châtaigne de mer* survives), and *button fish*
([Wiktionary: sea hedgehog](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sea_hedgehog);
[Wiktionary: sea urchin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sea_urchin)).

This is not an English quirk. German calls the animal **Seeigel**, literally "sea
hedgehog", *Igel* being the German hedgehog, and calls the heart urchin **Herzigel**
"heart-hedgehog". The Germanic family names the echinoderm after the hedgehog throughout
([Language Hat](https://languagehat.com/urchin/)).

### 5. How "hedgehog" became "child"

The pejorative application to a child appears in the sixteenth century and became common
after c. 1780, per the OED's first edition as quoted by Merriam-Webster
([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/urchin)). The intermediate
senses explain the slide: a hedgehog is small, spiny, nocturnal, and lives at the edge.

| Sense | Status | Note |
|---|---|---|
| hedgehog | archaic / dialect | the original 14c. meaning |
| sea urchin | current | recorded 1590s |
| mischievous elf, sometimes in hedgehog form | obsolete | 16c.–17c.; Shakespeare's goblin |
| carding-drum cylinder | technical | named for fancied resemblance to spines |
| ragged, mischievous child | current, old-fashioned register | 16c. onward, common after c. 1780 |
| small boy, youngster | current | weakest sense, mostly American |

Sources: [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/urchin),
[Collins](https://www.collinsdictionary.com/dictionary/english/urchin),
[Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/urchin),
[Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/urchin).

The chain is *thing that looks like a hedgehog* → *creature that behaves like one* →
*dirty, spiky, half-wild child*. Dictionary.com states the folk logic directly: the
children "got the name because at the time, they were so small, wild and many in number —
like hedgehogs" ([Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/urchin)).

### 6. The name that got dropped

A 19th-century Newfoundland name for sea urchins was **whore's eggs**. [unverified] —
the claim circulates in etymology discussion but I did not trace it to the *Dictionary of
Newfoundland English* through TinyFish, so treat it as second-hand until it is.

### 7. The scientific name says it again

Class **Echinoidea** Leske, 1778, from ἐχῖνος. The Greek word means "hedgehog" in the
strict sense and "spine" in the loose gloss; for this animal the two readings are the
same claim. The Latin-Greek name and the English name are independent coinages on one
sensory fact — the spines.

Aristotle's *Historia Animalium* describes the mouth apparatus, and the Loeb editor notes
that *echinus* "is a word applied to several other objects, including the hedgehog, the
sea-urchin, a large wide-mouthed jar, the hard core of beechnuts, chestnuts"
([Loeb Classical Library](https://www.loebclassics.com/view/aristotle-history_animals/1965/pb_LCL437.135.xml)).
The chewing organ is now **Aristotle's lantern**, described there and translated by
D'Arcy Thompson. Wikipedia records a dispute: the traditional account has Aristotle
comparing the structure to a horn lantern, while a recent reading argues the comparison
described the whole animal's shape as resembling the clay lamps of his time
([Wikipedia: Sea urchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_urchin)).

## Gotchas

- **The Greek and Latin branches are not cognates.** ἐχῖνος and ērīcius converge only at
  the PIE hedgehog/bristle root (\**h₁eǵʰis*, \**ǵʰers-* "to bristle"). Don't collapse
  the two lines.
- **etymonline.com blocks the fetcher** (`bot_blocked`), so its content is cited here
  only where a reachable source carries the same claim — Language Hat's comment thread
  and Merriam-Webster's word history cover that ground. A direct read is manual.
- **"Sea hedgehog" was the natural first English name**, not a modern back-formation. The
  modern coinage went the other way: *sea* added to an existing *urchin*.
- **French inverts the pattern.** *Oursin* derives from "bear fur", and the hedgehog word
  *hérisson* names only the land animal.
- **Register flips across senses.** Merriam-Webster marks the *child* sense
  old-fashioned and the *hedgehog* sense archaic — the reverse of the historical order.
