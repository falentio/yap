# Fog of War Board Games — Hidden Movement, Block Wargames, and Shared Blindness

## TL;DR

- **"Fog of war" is four different mechanics wearing one name.** A board game can hide *where* a unit is (hidden movement), *what* it is (block wargames), *who* a player is (hidden role), or *everything at once* (a shared blind state). Most "best fog of war" lists blur them together; the mechanic you actually want decides the pick ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog_of_war), [Command Post Games](https://www.commandpostgames.com/fog-of-war-board-games/)).
- **The genre's origin is *Scotland Yard* (1983)** — one player is Mr. X, hidden on a London map, tracked through a travel log that leaks a ticket type per move and five forced reveals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland_Yard_(board_game))).
- **The purest hidden-movement game is *Letters from Whitechapel*** (trail-of-clues deduction, best at 2, 90–150 min), and the modern one-vs-many favourites are *Mind MGMT*, *Sniper Elite*, *Beast*, and *Specter Ops* ([No Rolls Barred](https://norollsbarred.com/articles/now-you-see-me-a-guide-to-hidden-movement-games/), [Meeples Corner](https://meeplescorner.net/blogs/meeple-musings/what-are-hidden-movement-board-games-everything-you-need-to-know)).
- **The block wargame is the wargame answer to fog of war.** Wooden blocks stand facing each player, so unit *type* and *strength* stay hidden and rotate down as they take hits. Columbia Games shipped the first one, *Quebec 1759*, in 1972 ([Columbia Games](https://columbiagames.com/columbiablocksystem/)).
- **The rarest design is shared blindness** — the fog is not a hidden player but a table-wide state everyone is in together. *Captain Sonar* (real-time, both subs move at once), *The Fog of War* (Engelstein's decoy-and-intel WWII game), and this repo's own [MATI LAMPU](../reference/indonesian-blackout-bar-brawl.md) sit here ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Sonar), [Space-Biff!](https://spacebiff.com/2017/12/15/fog-of-war/)).
- **The single most important filter:** do you want to *be* the hidden player (one-vs-many chase), *hide your army's composition* (block wargame), or *have nobody able to see* (shared blindness)? Those are three different games that all get called "fog of war" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog_of_war)).

## Context

Question: *"fog of war board game"* — which games use it, what does the term actually mean at the table, and which one should a given group buy? ## What "fog of war" actually means

The term is military before it is ludic. The fog of war is *"the uncertainty in situational awareness experienced by participants in military operations"* — uncertainty about your own capability, the enemy's capability, and the enemy's intent. The metaphor traces to Clausewitz's *Vom Kriege* (1832): *"War is the realm of uncertainty; three quarters of the factors on which action in war is based are wrapped in a fog of greater or lesser uncertainty."* Clausewitz wrote "fog" but not the exact phrase; the first known use of "fog of war" in text is an 1836 poem, and the first explicit military definition is Col. Lonsdale Hale's 1896 book *The Fog of War*, describing *"the state of ignorance in which commanders frequently find themselves as regards the real strength and position, not only of their foes, but also of their friends."* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog_of_war))

That last clause — *not only of their foes, but also of their friends* — is the design target, and it is why fog-of-war games feel different from ordinary hidden-information games. A board game emulates it in one of four ways ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog_of_war), [Command Post Games](https://www.commandpostgames.com/fog-of-war-board-games/)):

1. **Hidden position** — you know what the pieces are, not where they are (hidden movement).
2. **Hidden identity/strength** — you know where the pieces are, not what they are (block wargames, *Stratego*).
3. **Hidden allegiance** — you don't know which side a player is on (hidden role).
4. **Shared blind state** — nobody has the information, and it is regenerated by the game each round (real-time or light-cycle fog).

The famous design illustration is the "hidden reserve" problem: an enemy HQ advances, and because a reserve card can hide troops behind it, *"for all we know, it could be anything… it could be nothing."* The defender must now spend a unit and a turn to go look — that lost tempo **is** the fog of war, made mechanical ([Command Post Games](https://www.commandpostgames.com/fog-of-war-board-games/)).

## Family A — Hidden movement (the one-vs-many chase)

One player moves in secret; everyone else deduces. The hidden player records moves privately — a notepad, a miniature board behind a screen, a card stack — while the hunters rely on clues, logic, and positioning ([Meeples Corner](https://meeplescorner.net/blogs/meeple-musings/what-are-hidden-movement-board-games-everything-you-need-to-know)).

| Dimension | Scotland Yard | Letters from Whitechapel | Fury of Dracula | Whitehall Mystery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Players | 3–6 | 2–6 (best 2) | 2–5 | 2–4 (best 2) |
| Playtime | ~45–60 min | 90–150 min | ~150 min (1–3 hr) | 45–60 min |
| Hidden player | Mr. X (criminal) | Jack the Ripper | Dracula | Jack the Ripper |
| Hunters | Detectives (co-op) | Police | Van Helsing + hunters | 3 investigators (fixed) |
| How hiding is tracked | Travel log + tickets; 5 forced reveals | Clue markers on a trail | Face-down location-card stack | Notepad + screen, one night |
| Long-range signal | Ticket type leaked per move | Fresh vs cold clue | Encounter cards / trail | Clue search on adjacent circles |
| Combat | None | None | Card-based duel | None |
| The hook | The genre's origin; teachable in 5 min | Trail deduction as a pure form | Night/day asymmetry; Dracula is weaker by day | Letters compressed into one night |

1. **Scotland Yard** — the 1983 Ravensburger game that *"laid the groundwork for modern hidden movement design"* and won Spiel des Jahres that year. Mr. X moves across a 199-station London map by taxi/bus/underground; the detectives see the *ticket type* he used each move (bus/underground/black) and get five forced position reveals, which is enough to triangulate if they are paying attention. 3–6 players, ~1 hour ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland_Yard_(board_game)), [Meeples Corner](https://meeplescorner.net/blogs/meeple-musings/what-are-hidden-movement-board-games-everything-you-need-to-know)).
2. **Letters from Whitechapel** — *"hidden movement at its purest: no fight mechanic, no special cards or levelling up, it's just one player moving in secret and everyone else trying to catch them."* Jack returns from a known murder site to a hidden base across four nights; police check numbered circles for clue markers. It distils to a superb 2-player cat-and-mouse, though it supports up to six ([No Rolls Barred](https://norollsbarred.com/articles/now-you-see-me-a-guide-to-hidden-movement-games/), [Meeples Corner](https://meeplescorner.net/blogs/meeple-musings/what-are-hidden-movement-board-games-everything-you-need-to-know)).
3. **Fury of Dracula** — *"took the promise of the OG of hidden movement, Scotland Yard, and added fighting, traps and lots and lots of bats."* One player prowls Europe as Dracula while 1–4 hunters pursue; a day/night cycle makes Dracula strong at night but restricts his movement to night, and combat is a card duel. Best with 2 players controlling two hunters each ([No Rolls Barred](https://norollsbarred.com/articles/now-you-see-me-a-guide-to-hidden-movement-games/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fury_of_Dracula)).
4. **Whitehall Mystery** — Fantasy Flight's 2017 re-tread of the *Letters* engine, tightened to a single night and four phases with a fixed three investigators. Jack gets a screen and a private map; the police *"hold up a lantern along a dark street"* — their clue search only reaches adjacent numbered circles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehall_Mystery), [Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/whitehall-mystery/)).

### The modern one-vs-many wave

| Dimension | Mind MGMT | Sniper Elite | Beast | Specter Ops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Players | 1–5 | 1–4 (best 2) | 2–4 base (best 3); 2–5 with *The Great Hunt* | 2–5 |
| Playtime | ~60 min | 40–70 min | **~30 min per player** (2p ≈ 60, 4p ≈ 120) | 60–120 min |
| Hidden player | The Recruiter | The Allied sniper | The Beast | The A.R.K. agent |
| Hunters | Rogue Agents | German squads (3×3) | Hunters | Raxxon hunters |
| Deduction system | Ask about symbols; reveal a foot, then a brain | Check squares / line of sight; shots make noise | Search a location → trail appears; attack reveals | Line-of-sight detection; hunter vehicle |
| Novel twist | 14-match unlock campaign (7 modules per side) | Sniper bag-draw targeting; shooting gives you away | Beast drafts secret direction cards; hunts to grow | Hidden agent vs mutants with powers |
| Catch | Rulebook is notoriously bad | Playing the hunters can feel *"adrift in a pitch-black sea"* | Reported balance swing between beast/hunter matchups | Longest of the four; can run 2 hours |

1. **Mind MGMT: The Psychic Espionage "Game."** — the reviewer's *"favourite hidden movement game"*: the Recruiter moves in secret on a dry-erase mini-board and answers symbol queries, and the box carries a 14-match unlock folio where the *loser* gets a new module. 1–5 players, ~60 min ([Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/mind-mgmt-the-psychic-espionage-game/)).
2. **Sniper Elite: The Board Game** — one sniper vs 1–3 German squad commanders; squads move/check/shoot, the sniper cannot pass through occupied squares, and *"if the sniper ever takes a shot… their location will be identified with a noise marker."* Its best moments are the feint — taking first shots near the *wrong* objective ([The Players' Aid](https://theplayersaid.com/2024/03/22/best-3-games-with-hidden-movement/), [Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/sniper-elite-the-board-game/)).
3. **Beast** — a hidden-movement game where hiding comes in *bursts*: the Beast must hunt to win, and hunting reveals it. Card-drafting at the start of each round is the real game, and both sides level up abilities overnight; dead hunters are resurrected, so nobody sits out. The base box is 2–4 players and the publisher now lists 2–5 (with *The Great Hunt*), at **~30 minutes per player** — so a 2-player game is about an hour and a 4-player game closer to two ([No Rolls Barred](https://norollsbarred.com/articles/now-you-see-me-a-guide-to-hidden-movement-games/), [Zatu](https://zatu.com/blogs/reviews/beast-review), [Studio Midhall](https://www.studiomidhall.com/)).
4. **Specter Ops** — a lone A.R.K. agent vs 2+ genetically enhanced Raxxon hunters; the agent marks moves secretly and is revealed only through a hunter's line of sight, while hunters drive a motion-detecting vehicle. 2–5 players, 60–120 min ([Plaid Hat Games](https://www.plaidhatgames.com/board-games/specter-ops/)).

> **Also in this family:** *They Come Unseen* (NATO subs vs Soviet hunter-killers, asymmetric hidden deployment) and *Bomber Command: The Night Raids* (both players plot secret flight paths on planning sheets) ([The Players' Aid](https://theplayersaid.com/2024/03/22/best-3-games-with-hidden-movement/)); *City of the Great Machine* inverts the norm — *every* player except one is hidden — and runs 1–4 players, 45–90 min ([No Rolls Barred](https://norollsbarred.com/articles/now-you-see-me-a-guide-to-hidden-movement-games/), [Crowd Games](https://crowdgames.us/our-products/city-of-the-great-machine/)).

## Family B — Block wargames (hidden strength)

The block wargame hides *what a unit is*, not where it is. Pieces are wooden blocks that stand up facing their owner, so the opponent sees a shape and a location but not the type or strength; when a unit takes damage the block rotates, so **step reduction** and hidden strength are the same mechanism. The genre started in 1972 when Tom Dalgliesh and Lance Gutteridge replaced die-cut counters with wooden blocks while designing *Quebec 1759* for Columbia Games ([Columbia Games](https://columbiagames.com/columbiablocksystem/)).

| Dimension | Hammer of the Scots | Julius Caesar | Sekigahara | Fields of Despair |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Players | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1–2 (solo aid included; 2p is best) |
| Playtime | 120–180 min | 90–180 min (1.5–3 hr) | ~180 min | campaign 7–8 hr (short scenarios ~2 hr) |
| Setting | Wars of Scottish Independence, 1297–1314 | Roman civil war, 49–45 BC | Japan, 1600 | Western Front, WWI |
| Hidden info | Block type + strength | Block strength; hidden event cards | Block strength + loyalty | Block strength; **dummy blocks**; 0–20 value range |
| Signature mechanic | Card-driven activation + step reduction | Card-driven Move/Levy/Event; hidden battle strength | Loyalty checks; no dice in battle | Air reconnaissance removes the fog; dummy blocks |
| The catch | Long for its weight | One scenario, one setup | Full-evening game | Full campaign is a multi-session project |

1. **Hammer of the Scots** — the Columbia entry-level classic; card-driven activation plus step reduction, 2 players, 120–180 min ([Columbia Games](https://columbiagames.com/columbiablocksystem/hammer-of-the-scots-block-wargame/)).
2. **Julius Caesar** — a card-driven block game across the whole Mediterranean where *"the strength of units is hidden till the last moment before clash"*, so a battle is a genuine surprise; teachable in 10 minutes, 2 players ([The Boardgames Chronicle](https://theboardgameschronicle.com/2022/11/06/review-julius-caesar/)).
3. **Sekigahara: The Unification of Japan** — a 2-player, ~3-hour block game praised as *"a lot of 'game' in 3 hours"* with simple mechanics; a recurring answer to "best fog of war mechanic" because blocks stay hidden until the moment of battle ([GMT Games](https://www.gmtgames.com/p-972-sekigahara-5th-printing.aspx)).
4. **Fields of Despair: France 1914–1918** — the design that pushes block fog to its limit: block combat values run from **0 (dummy blocks) to 20**, so one block can hide the strength that used to take four or five blocks to conceal, and blocks **stay hidden even when enemies share the hex** until someone spends air reconnaissance or attacks. The catch is scale — the full campaign ran two sittings, ~7–8 hours. It ships a solitaire play aid (so it is listed 1–2 players), but owner reviews call the solo rules awkward — *"the solo game is a bit awkward to work out"* — and 2-player is the intended mode ([The Players' Aid](https://theplayersaid.com/2020/12/04/best-3-games-with-blocks/), [The Boardgames Chronicle](https://theboardgameschronicle.com/2024/07/13/fields-of-despair-france-1914-1918-impressions-after-the-full-campaign/), [GMT Games](https://www.gmtgames.com/p-686-fields-of-despair-2nd-printing.aspx)).

> **Why blocks, not counters:** a traditional wargame gives both players a "God's eye view" of every unit's type and strength. Blocks restore the commander's actual uncertainty — and, because a block is a physical dial, they give hidden strength and attrition in one component ([Columbia Games](https://columbiagames.com/columbiablocksystem/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog_of_war)).

## Family C — Shared blindness (nobody can see)

The rarest family: the fog is not a hidden player but a table-wide state that changes on its own, so everyone is blind *together*.

| Dimension | Captain Sonar | The Fog of War (Engelstein) | MATI LAMPU (this repo) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Players | 2–8 (two teams of 4; real-time wants 6–8) | 2 | 2–6 |
| Playtime | ~45 min | 90–120 min | ~30–45 min (design target) |
| What is hidden | The enemy sub's position | Province + card strength of every planned operation | Every player's position beyond their own vision radius |
| Fog driver | Real-time simultaneous movement; the captain announces directions aloud | Secret op placement on an Operations Wheel + decoy cards | A public light die rolled every round sets everyone's vision radius |
| Counter-play | A Radio Operator listens and plots the enemy path | Intel tokens peek at half a stack (and reshuffled, so even the enemy isn't sure what you saw) | A torch buys range but makes you visible to the whole table |
| Combat | Torpedoes / mines / surfacing | Abstract battle resolution; supply cut-offs | Opposed d6, weapons break, knockdowns drop loot |
| Source | [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Sonar) | [Space-Biff!](https://spacebiff.com/2017/12/15/fog-of-war/), [The Players' Aid](https://theplayersaid.com/2016/12/21/interview-with-geoff-engelstein-designer-of-the-fog-of-war-by-stronghold-games/) | [MATI LAMPU](../reference/indonesian-blackout-bar-brawl.md) |

1. **Captain Sonar** — *"turns hidden movement into real-time submarine warfare for 2–8 players."* Two crews of up to four sit either side of a divider; the captain announces every move aloud, and the opposing Radio Operator reconstructs the path. Real-time mode has both subs moving at once. **Real-time wants 6 or 8 players** (roles double up at 4 and 6); with 2–5 you must play turn-by-turn mode, and reviewers call real-time below 6 unbalanced. At 8 it was described as *"an almost peerless example of the medium"* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Sonar), [Captain Sonar rules PDF](https://northstreetgames.com/images/Rulebooks/Captain-Sonar-Rules-v5.pdf), [Meeples Corner](https://meeplescorner.net/blogs/meeple-musings/what-are-hidden-movement-board-games-everything-you-need-to-know)).
2. **The Fog of War** (Geoff Engelstein, Stronghold Games, 2016) — a 2-player WWII game that is explicitly *Operation Bodyguard*: there are **no unit pieces on the map**, only control markers and out-of-supply tokens. Each side secretly lays a province plus action cards on an Operations Wheel and fills provinces with decoys, so *"you can form entire dummy invasions with all the punch of a light breeze."* Intel tokens let you peek at half a stack — and the stack reshuffles afterward, so *"even your enemy won't be entirely certain whether you've figured out what he's up to."* 2 players, 90–120 min ([Space-Biff!](https://spacebiff.com/2017/12/15/fog-of-war/), [The Players' Aid](https://theplayersaid.com/2016/12/21/interview-with-geoff-engelstein-designer-of-the-fog-of-war-by-stronghold-games/)).
3. **MATI LAMPU (Blackout Bar Brawl)** — this repo's own design, and the cleanest example of the family: a public **light die** rolled every round sets *everyone's* vision radius at once, so the fog is a shared resource rather than a hidden player. A torch extends your vision to three tiles and paints you for the whole table; noise markers steer an NPC security guard toward whoever is loudest. Its own simulation showed the **map topology, not the light radii**, was the balance lever ([MATI LAMPU](../reference/indonesian-blackout-bar-brawl.md)).

## Family D — Hidden identity (the edge case)

Included for completeness, because lists conflate it with the rest: this hides *allegiance*, not position or strength.

- **Stratego** (1946) — 2 players, 40 pieces each, hidden ranks; the original mass-market hidden-strength game and the ancestor of the block wargame. 10 min to 1.5 hr ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratego), [Wikipedia — fog of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog_of_war)).
- **Hidden-role/social-deduction games** — *The Resistance: Avalon*, *One Night Ultimate Werewolf*, *Secret Hitler*, *Blood on the Clocktower* — hide who a player is, not where a unit is. See [Board Games Like Coup: Rebellion G54](./board-games-like-coup-rebellion-g54.md) for that family ranked.
- **Star Wars: Rebellion** — a hybrid: the Rebel player secretly picks the hidden base system and the Empire searches with probe cards, layered over a full 2–4 player, 3–4 hr strategy game ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars:_Rebellion_(board_game)), [Meeples Corner](https://meeplescorner.net/blogs/meeple-musings/what-are-hidden-movement-board-games-everything-you-need-to-know)).

## Resolved since first draft

- **Beast's playtime** — the conflict ("under an hour" vs ~90 min) was a player-count artifact, not a contradiction. The publisher lists **30 minutes per player**, so a 2-player game is ~1 hour and a 4-player game ~2 hours; Zatu's "under an hour" is the low end ([Studio Midhall](https://www.studiomidhall.com/), [Zatu](https://zatu.com/blogs/reviews/beast-review)).
- **Fields of Despair solo** — confirmed: the box ships a **1 Solitaire Play Aid** and the game is listed **1–2 players**, though owner reviews call the solo rules awkward and 2-player is the intended mode ([GMT Games](https://www.gmtgames.com/p-686-fields-of-despair-2nd-printing.aspx)).
- **Captain Sonar falloff** — quantified: real-time mode wants **6 or 8** players (roles double up at 4 and 6); 2–5 forces turn-by-turn mode, and real-time below 6 is called unbalanced ([Captain Sonar rules PDF](https://northstreetgames.com/images/Rulebooks/Captain-Sonar-Rules-v5.pdf), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Sonar)).

## Open Questions

- **BoardGameGeek stats were bot-blocked** on 2026-10-11, so BGG complexity weights and rankings for every title here are unverified. The *Whitehall Mystery* player count/playtime came from a search-result snippet of its BGG page, not a direct fetch.
- Whether *The Fog of War* (Engelstein) ever got the mooted **Pacific Theater** extension is unknown — the designer discussed it in 2016, and a 2026-10-11 search turned up **no release evidence**, so treat it as never shipped pending a primary source ([The Players' Aid](https://theplayersaid.com/2016/12/21/interview-with-geoff-engelstein-designer-of-the-fog-of-war-by-stronghold-games/)).
- **Beast's player count moved** — the base box is 2–4, but Studio Midhall's own site now says 2–5 (the *The Great Hunt* expansion adds a fifth player). Which count the "best at 3" consensus applies to is [unverified].
